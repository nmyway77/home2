Common sense tells us that more choice is always better. If a store offers twenty kinds of cereal, surely customers will be happier than if it offers only three. Yet a growing body of research suggests that this assumption is often wrong. When people face too many options, they frequently end up choosing nothing at all.

In a well-known experiment, psychologists set up a tasting table in a supermarket. On some days the table displayed twenty-four flavors of jam; on other days, only six. The larger display attracted more shoppers, who stopped to look and taste. However, when it came to actually buying, the results were striking. Only about three percent of those who visited the twenty-four-flavor table made a purchase, while nearly thirty percent of the visitors to the six-flavor table bought a jar.

Researchers explain this pattern in terms of mental effort. Comparing a small number of options is manageable, but comparing dozens requires so much energy that people feel paralyzed. They also worry more about making the wrong decision, since every choice means giving up many attractive alternatives. A similar effect was observed in a study of company retirement plans: for every ten additional investment options offered to employees, the rate of participation dropped by roughly two percent, even though joining the plan was clearly in their financial interest.

This does not mean that choice itself is bad. Rather, it suggests that the way choices are presented matters enormously. Businesses and institutions that carefully limit and organize their options may serve people better than those that simply offer everything and let customers sort it out for themselves.
