In 1977, psychologists1 Lynn Hasher and David Goldstein asked a group of college students how certain2 they were that sixty general-knowledge trivia-like statements3 were either true or false. The students were asked4 to evaluate the sixty statements three times, at two-week intervals5. Crucially6, only twenty of the statements were7 repeated8 each time; the other forty were new. The researchers then compared9 the ‘truth-ratings’ of the repeated versus non-repeated statements. What10 they found was astonishing11. Regardless12 of whether the statement was true or false, belief13 in the truth of the claim went14 up15 as a function16 of repetition17. In other words, the more18 often you hear a statement, the more ‘true’ it sounds19. This became20 known as the illusory21 truth effect.

Something similar happens with advertising slogans. Back in 1977, the psychologists Lynn Hasher and David Goldstein presented a group of college students with sixty trivia-style statements and asked how confident they were that each one was true or false.
