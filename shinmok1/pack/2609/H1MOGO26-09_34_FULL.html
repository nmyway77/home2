If our bodies know best, why are they1 so easily2 injured when we do something so seemingly3 natural as running? On the one hand, running seems very easy4, but that is only because our bodies are so very good at it5. The most powerful supercomputer6 on the planet cannot administer the enormous7 number of computations it takes8 to run, untethered, on two legs. Wherever our running intelligence9 is, running is not all conscious10 to us and is so11 complicated that only the T-1000 Terminators of our imagination12 are able to do it. Because our running knowledge is buried13 so deeply within muscle fibres14, cells and our DNA, we have to accept that we cannot fully15 explain its complexities16. That doesn’t mean our bodies don’t know how to do it. We may feel our way towards understanding the alchemy17 of running by learning how to use our senses, getting them to fire18, and seeing19 what they are capable of, and accepting20 that our bodies know more about running than we ever will21.

1If our bodies know best, why are they so easily injured when we do something so seemingly natural as running?

그렇다고 우리 몸이 달리는 법을 모른다는 뜻은 아니다.

34-1
복합 <보기> 판별 (A)~(D)
3.3점
다음 글에 대한 <보기>의 설명 중 틀린 것만을 있는 대로 고른 것은?
If our bodies really do know best, why do they get hurt so easily when we do something as apparently natural as running? Running looks effortless, yet that impression exists only because our bodies handle it so well. Not even the most powerful supercomputer on Earth could manage the vast number of calculations required to run freely on two legs. Wherever our running intelligence may be located, running is not something we are fully aware of, and it is complicated enough (ⓐ) [that / what] only the T-1000 Terminators of our imagination could pull it off. Since this knowledge lies buried deep in our muscle fibres, our cells and our DNA, we must admit that we will never be able to explain it completely. That does not mean our bodies are ignorant of how to run. We can grope toward an understanding of running’s strange chemistry by learning to use our senses, getting them working, finding out what they can do—and by admitting that our bodies will always understand running better than we do.
<보 기>
(A) The T-1000 Terminators are mentioned as an example of real machines that can already run on two legs as well as humans.
(B) ‘that’ is the right choice for (ⓐ), since ‘complicated enough … that’ introduces a result clause.
(C) ‘That does not mean our bodies are ignorant of how to run.’ means that although we cannot explain running, our bodies still know how to do it.
(D) In the summary ‘Running, which our bodies perform with ease, are too complex for us to explain fully’, the verb ‘are’ agrees with its subject.
①(A), (B)
②(B), (C)
③(C), (D)
④(A), (D)
⑤(A), (C), (D)

[A]와 [B]를 읽고 이해한 것으로 가장 적절하지 않은 것은?
[A] The most powerful supercomputer on the planet cannot administer the enormous number of computations it takes to run, untethered, on two legs. The rider does not understand balance; she simply has it, and the surest way to lose it is to start thinking about it.
①Both texts suggest that a skill the body performs with ease may be impossible for us to put into words.
②The supercomputer in [A] and the self-balancing bike in [B] both stand for the enormous machine effort needed to copy what the body does naturally.
③[B] claims that thinking carefully about balance is the best way to improve it, which matches [A]’s advice to use our senses.
④[A] locates running knowledge in muscles, cells and DNA, while [B] describes balance as tiny corrections the rider cannot write down.
⑤Neither text says that the body’s inability to explain a skill means the body does not know how to perform it.
