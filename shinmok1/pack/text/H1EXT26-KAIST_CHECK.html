Other than humans, a species of bats 1)[ known / knowing ] as the “greater sac-winged bats” are the only mammals to engage in infant “talk,” or babble. About 2)  inches long, the greater sac-winged bats are nocturnal, sleeping through much of the day and remaining awake at night. Though they live together in groups, they keep enough distance between themselves, 3)[ which / that ] enables researchers to record 4) (separate) the sounds each bat makes. This is 5)[ crucial / trivial ] to researchers who study the babbling of these greater sac-winged bats. Recently, a group of researchers tried to analyze in detail the babbling behavior of these infant bats, also 6)[ called / calling ] bat pups, and to compare it with human infant babbling.
Analyzing the recordings of 7)  “babbling bursts,” which averaged about seven minutes but sometimes lasted as long as 8)  minutes, of 20 bat pups from two bat communities in Costa Rica and Panama, the researchers found that the sounds the bat pups made 9)[ were / was ] similar to 10)[ those / that ] of human infants in their 11) (repeat) of syllables, the 12) (rhythm) nature of the babbling, and the universality of babbling behavior. Other similarities with the babbling of human infants included the early start of babbling, the long sequences of sounds, and the fact that the pups didn’t need any stimulus from other bats — like human babies, they just babbled on, 13) (gradual) acquiring more sounds.
Why do bat pups babble then? The researchers believe that babbling helps the bat pups prepare for and learn about the elements in courtship songs 14)[ sung / singing ] by adult bats. When the female pups learn through babbling the basic building blocks of a courtship song, for example, such knowledge later makes them 15)[ better / worse ] judges of male song when they have grown beyond their infancy. Adult males, on the other hand, compete 16)[ intensely / intense ] to attract females using their bat-song, the components of 17)[ which / them ] they learned about in their infancy while babbling. Ultimately, the females choose which males they prefer, and the males constantly court them in a kind of talent competition. Such common characteristics may yield 18)[ valuable / useless ] insights for better understanding the essential nature and development of vocal communication not only of humans but, ultimately, of non-human species as well.
