The wish to build a machine that thinks and decides as a person does is centuries old. Greek myth already told of robots and artificial beings — Pandora, who let loose the world's ills, among them — and literature kept the wish alive, most famously in Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. The wish turned scientific in 1950, when the British mathematician Alan Turing asked whether a machine could think and reason like a human and designed a test to find out whether it could think on its own. A few years afterward, John McCarthy of MIT gave the field its name, “artificial intelligence,” in place of the older “automata studies.” Ever since, the field has meant building machines programmed to think as humans do, fitted by their makers with the ability to reason and to learn.
