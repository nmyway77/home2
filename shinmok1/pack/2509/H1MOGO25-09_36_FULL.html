윗글의 내용과 일치하지 않는 것은?
The desert tortoise beats Death Valley's heat by simply staying out of it. It sleeps through the winter and passes most of the summer underground, so that well over nine-tenths of its life is spent motionless. It comes to the surface mainly after a good rain, and then it gets busy, eating plants and scooping out holes that catch rainwater. What carries it through the long months of sleep, though, is its remarkable bladder, which works as a storage tank from which water can be drawn back into the body — enough to last a full year without a single drink of fresh water. That is why park rangers ask visitors not to lift a tortoise off the road: a frightened tortoise empties its bladder, and with it goes the water it cannot afford to lose.

Rather than endure Death Valley's heat, the desert tortoise avoids it. Hibernating in winter and staying below ground for much of summer, it spends more than 90 percent of its life without moving. After a good rain it emerges to feed on plants and dig holes for collecting water. During its long dormancy it depends on an unusually sophisticated bladder that holds water for reabsorption, which lets it survive a whole year with no fresh water at all. Because that bladder is vital, rangers warn visitors against helping tortoises across roads; a tortoise that is picked up becomes so frightened that it empties its bladder and loses its reserves.
