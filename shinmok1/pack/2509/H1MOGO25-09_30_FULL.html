We are a species that grew up in an age of scarcity and danger, and that history leaves our instinct to share and cooperate in an awkward position once goods are plentiful and threats are rare. Owning less tends to make people more willing to share what they own. Some wandering tribes possess very little, yet they share gladly, because sharing serves them: a traveler who stumbles onto their camp is welcomed, fed, and sheltered. Kindness is part of it, but the deeper reason is survival — they know that one day they may be the ones on the road, hungry and without shelter. The irony is that greater wealth brings higher fences, tighter security to keep others out, and a weaker wish to share. Wanting more, together with less everyday contact with ordinary people, begins to cut us off from how things really are.

윗글의 내용과 일치하지 않는 것은?
Human beings were born, as a species, into a world where resources were thin and dangers thick. Under those conditions sharing and cooperation came naturally; in a world of plenty and safety they become far less simple. The rule still holds that the less we own, the more readily we share it. Certain nomadic peoples own almost nothing and still open their homes to strangers, offering food and welcome — not merely because they are good-natured, but because their own survival rests on the same custom. The reverse is equally true: the more we accumulate, the higher the walls we build, the more elaborate the locks, and the smaller our appetite for giving anything away. That appetite for more, joined with shrinking contact with the “common folk,” breeds a kind of blindness to reality.

That generosity, however, is not simple kindness.
Sharing comes most easily to those who have the least. ( ① ) Nomadic peoples who own almost nothing will still take a stranger in, offering food and a place to sleep.
