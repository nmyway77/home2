2.7점
윗글의 내용과 일치하지 않는 것은?
Picture yourself riding a bicycle along a flat road and then simply ceasing to pedal. Nothing is pushing you forward any more, and you slow down little by little. To stop more sharply, you squeeze the brakes; because they change your motion, they must be applying a force as they grip the rims. That force is friction, which works against motion — backwards, in this case. Brakes or no brakes, other friction is always at work: the wheels rub on their axles, and the air itself resists you, pressing you backwards as you move forwards. Look at a cart and the same idea applies; most of its friction comes from axles rubbing on the body as they turn.

2.6점
윗글의 내용과 일치하지 않는 것은?
A cyclist who stops pedalling on level ground loses speed gradually, because no force is now driving the bicycle forward and other forces are holding it back. Applying the brakes shortens the stopping distance: the brake pads grip and rub on the wheel rims, and that rubbing exerts a force on bicycle and rider. Physicists call the force friction; it acts in the direction opposite to motion and so slows moving things down. Even with the brakes off, friction between wheels and axles and the resistance of the air keep dragging the rider backwards.

조건 · 보기
The brakes are not the only source of that force, however.
Stop pedalling on a flat road and the bicycle slows, gradually at first. ( ① ) Squeeze the brakes and it slows much faster, because the pads grip the rims and apply a force against your motion. ( ② ) That force is friction, which always pushes the opposite way from the way you are moving. ( ③ ) The wheels rub on their axles, and the air presses against you as you ride, both slowing you down even with the brakes untouched. ( ④ ) A cart works the same way, most of its friction coming from the axles turning against the body. ( ⑤ ) Friction, in short, is everywhere a moving thing touches something else.
