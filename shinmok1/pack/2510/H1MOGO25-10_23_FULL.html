Rome was said to have been1 a melting pot2 from the very start. The historian Livy claimed the city’s original3 population was comprised of immigrants flooding4 in from all directions, attracted5 by Romulus’s deliberate6 policy of nondiscrimination7. It was this initial openness8, Livy asserts, that9 laid the foundations10 for the later strength11 and success of the city. Romans described their city as multicultural12 in the generations after its foundation. Tradition held that only a minority13 of the city’s legendary14 kings were15 Roman-born, with the others all arriving16 as immigrants before being chosen17 for the throne for their virtues18 and merits. As the empire expanded19 across three continents, Rome eagerly20 adopted new cultural influences and absorbed incoming groups — perhaps a little too eagerly for some, who21, like the poet Juvenal, complained about the rapid rate of cultural change.

According to tradition, Rome was a mixture of peoples from its earliest days. The historian Livy wrote that the first Romans were immigrants who had poured in from every direction, drawn by Romulus’s intentional policy of treating all newcomers alike. In Livy’s view, it was precisely this early willingness to take people in that made the city strong and successful later on. In the generations that followed its founding, Romans themselves spoke of their city as a place of many cultures. Tradition also claimed that most of the city’s legendary kings had not been born in Rome; they had come as immigrants and were raised to the throne because of their character and achievements. Later, as Roman rule spread over three continents, the city took in new customs and new peoples with enthusiasm — too much enthusiasm, in the eyes of some, such as the poet Juvenal, who grumbled that the culture was changing far too fast.
