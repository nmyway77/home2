The technical term often used1 to describe animals’ judgement2 of numbers is the approximate3 number system. What it does not provide4 is5 precision6. It shows — and this is the same in every species tested — a characteristic7 pattern of errors, with discrimination becoming8 less accurate9 as the quantities get bigger10. Rhesus monkeys can tell one from two, two from three, three from four, four from five ... but start to fail11 from five upwards. Rats that12 learned13 to press a lever a given number of times, from four up to twenty-four, became markedly14 less and less precise15 in their responses as the number increased16: by the top end of the range they would merely produce a spread of numbers around the target17. It is a common observation18 that when testing19 the accuracy of animals’ number sense, the size20 of the numbers matters21.

Human beings are not so different when they cannot count. Shown two dots and then three, almost anyone says32 without hesitation which group is larger. Shown twenty-three dots and then twenty-six, most people hesitate, and their answers begin33 to scatter on either side of the truth. The gap between the two numbers is the same in both cases, but the bigger numbers blur together. Our quick sense of quantity, which34 works so well for small groups, loses its edge as the groups grow.
